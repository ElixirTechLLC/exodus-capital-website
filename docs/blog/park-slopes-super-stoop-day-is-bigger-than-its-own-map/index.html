Park Slope Super Stoop Day: What to Know Before You Go

October 15, 2026

Pull up the official map for this year's Super Stoop Day and you'll see dots scattered across Park Slope, each one a stoop or sidewalk that someone registered in advance with the Park Slope Civic Council. What the map will not show you is the stoop three doors down that isn't on it at all, the one where a neighbor decided that morning to drag out a folding table, a box of paperbacks, and a sign written in Sharpie. Last year, that gap between what got registered and what actually happened turned out to be the most honest thing about the whole day.

Later this month, on Saturday, September 26, from 11 a.m. to 4 p.m., Park Slope's stoops turn into a neighborhood-wide sidewalk market for the second year running. If you missed it last September, or if you caught it and are wondering whether it's worth clearing your Saturday for again, here's what the numbers from year one actually tell you, and what to expect when you walk it this time.

What Super Stoop Day Actually Is

The Park Slope Civic Council runs Super Stoop Day as a single-day, neighborhood-wide event where residents, schools, and businesses turn their front steps and sidewalks into pop-up shops. Anyone can register a stoop online ahead of time and note what they'll have on offer. Registered stoops then show up on a digital map and a printed one, so you can plan a route instead of wandering blind.

Registration for this year's event closes September 20. If Saturday brings rain, the rain date is Sunday, September 27, with updates posted to the Civic Council's site and Instagram the morning of.

None of this costs anything to attend. It costs nothing to register a stoop either, which is part of why it grew as fast as it did.

What the First Year's Numbers Actually Show

Here's the detail that matters if you're deciding how seriously to take this. In the run-up to the inaugural 2025 event, coverage from Macaroni Kid Brooklyn NW and Park Slope Pulse both put the registered count at roughly 200 groups and stoops. That was the number as of the week before the event, pulled straight from the sign-up form.

By the time the Civic Council promoted this year's second event, the number they used to describe last year's turnout was different: over 250 participating households. That's not a rounding error or a correction. It's the gap between who signed up in advance and who actually showed up on the sidewalk that day. Park Slope Pulse's own coverage flagged this in real time, noting that beyond the mapped stoops, attendees should "look out for even more spontaneous pop-up stoops along the way."

Fifty-plus households joined without ever appearing on a map. That tells you something about how this particular neighborhood organizes itself. The official registration count is a floor, not a ceiling. People here don't need an app to know that Saturday is stoop sale day. They find out from a neighbor, a flyer taped to a lamppost, or simply by seeing three blocks of activity and deciding to join in. If you're walking it this year expecting the map to be the whole event, plan to be pleasantly wrong.

What You'll Actually Find on the Sidewalk

Based on last year's turnout, expect the usual texture of a neighborhood clearing out its closets: books, kids' clothes, toys, plants, handmade crafts, furniture that's too good to trash but not quite right for the next apartment. Much of it is priced to move or given away outright.

Food is its own category. Last year, more than two dozen households and small vendors sold food and drink directly from their stoops, ranging from lemonade stands to vegan soups to home-baked goods. That's not a farmers market vendor list. That's your actual neighbors deciding to cook for the day.

If you want a starting point rather than a random entry into the map, the Civic Council staffed information tables last year at the corner of President Street and Prospect Park West, with a second table at 4th Street Plaza running from 11:30 a.m. to 2 p.m. Both spots handed out printed maps for anyone who didn't want to navigate from a phone screen while carrying a lamp they just bought for three dollars.

The Organization Running This Has Been at It Since 1896

It's worth knowing who's actually behind Super Stoop Day, because it explains why a brand-new event scaled so quickly. The Park Slope Civic Council has been organizing around this neighborhood since 1896, and its current mission statement still centers on promoting "the health, vitality and character of the neighborhood." This isn't a startup nonprofit testing out programming. It's an organization with more than a century of practice getting residents to show up for each other.

Super Stoop Day sits on the Civic Council's calendar alongside older traditions built on the same instinct. Food For Thought, the Council's scholarship fundraiser held this year on October 15, has for years brought together neighborhood restaurants for a tasting event that funds college scholarships for graduating seniors, a program the Council has run in some form since the early 1960s. Donna Stein, the Civic Council's current president, has described finding her own way into the organization through that exact event after moving to Park Slope in 2019; in Park Slope Pulse, she said of the neighborhood, "I knew I had to find a way to make it feel like home."

There's also a Fall Meeting on September 17, a week and a half before Super Stoop Day, which functions as the Council's regular check-in on neighborhood business. Together, these three events in a four-week window are less a coincidence of scheduling and more a snapshot of how much of this neighborhood's social calendar still runs through one 130-year-old civic organization rather than a phone app or a listserv.

Planning Your Saturday

A few practical notes if you're walking this year's event rather than just registering a stoop:

  • The core hours are 11 a.m. to 4 p.m., but plenty of stoops open early or run late depending on how much stock they've got left.
  • Cash still helps. A lot of these are one-person operations without a card reader, especially the food stands.
  • Bring a bag. Furniture and larger items move fast in the first hour, and you don't want to be the person carrying a lamp four blocks with no way to hold it.
  • Check the Civic Council's site or Instagram the morning of if the weather looks uncertain. The rain date is the next day, Sunday the 27th.
  • Don't skip a block just because it looks quiet on the map. Some of the best finds last year weren't registered anywhere.

The Map Is a Starting Point, Not the Record

The real story of Super Stoop Day isn't the map, it's the fifty-plus households who showed up without bothering to register on it. That's the same instinct that's kept the Park Slope Civic Council running events since before most of these brownstones had electricity. A digital map can tell you where to start walking. It can't tell you what you'll actually find, because half of what makes the day worth doing never got logged anywhere official in the first place.
